=== lesson C1 ===

  C1  Relative motion - the only plot that answers the question
  ---------------------------------------------------------------

  You cannot steer a ship by watching how a contact moves over the ground.
  What matters is how she moves relative to YOU, and in relative motion
  she does something very simple: she travels in a straight line.

  Put yourself at the centre and plot where she is relative to you every
  three minutes. Those marks fall on a straight line. Extend it. Where it
  passes you is the CPA, and which side of you it passes is the whole
  question - ahead, or astern.

  28100 yd across                  |
                                   |
                         1.        |
                          ..       |
                           2..     |
                             .3    |
                              ..   |
                               ..4 |
                                 .^^^
                                  .5
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
  Y = you, head up.  1 to 5 = her, three minutes apart.  * = CPA

  Two things follow, and everything else in this section comes out of them:

    1. Because the line is straight, the bearing sweeps one way and one
       way only. It can never reverse. Whatever it is doing now, it will
       go on doing until somebody alters course.
    2. If the line goes through the centre, the bearing does not change
       at all - and that is a collision.

=== lesson C2 ===

  C2  Drift toward the bow, and drift away from it
  --------------------------------------------------

  This is the whole trade, and it is one sentence:

    A bearing drawing AWAY from your bow will pass astern of you.
    A bearing drawing TOWARD your bow will cross ahead of you.

  On the left, drawing left: she goes down your side and astern.
  On the left, drawing right: she is going to cross your bow.
  On the right, drawing right: astern.  On the right, drawing left: ahead.

  This is not a rule of thumb. It is a consequence of the straight line in
  C1: the bearing sweeps one way, so a contact drawing away from your bow
  can never come back to it. There are no exceptions and no edge cases.

  Which is why one of the two is a report and the other is a warning.
  Drawing away, the miss is behind you and it is getting bigger. Drawing
  toward, the miss is being built in front of your bow - and it shrinks to
  nothing the moment the drift slows. A contact drawing slowly toward your
  bow is a collision that has not finished making up its mind.

  Say it as a pair, always, because one word without the other is useless:

    "On the left, drawing left."   "On the left, drawing right."

=== lesson C3 ===

  C3  Constant bearing, decreasing range
  ----------------------------------------

  Rule 7(d)(i): risk of collision shall be deemed to exist if the compass
  bearing of an approaching vessel does not appreciably change.

  Deemed. Not estimated, not assessed - deemed. You do not get to argue
  with it, and you do not wait for the range to confirm it.

  23800 yd across                  |
                                   |
                          1.       |
                           2.      |
                            ..     |
                             3..   |
                               4.  |
                                .. |
                                 5^^^
                                  .*
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
                                   |
  A steady bearing. The line goes through the middle. That is all a
  collision is: two ships keeping the same bearing on each other.

  But read the second half of the rule, which is the half that kills people:

    7(d)(ii): such risk may sometimes exist even when an appreciable
    bearing change is evident, particularly when approaching a very
    large vessel or a tow, or when approaching a vessel at close range.

  Three reasons, and they are all about the ship being bigger than the dot
  you are plotting:

    1. A very large vessel. You take the bearing of her bridge.
       She is 300 metres long. Her bow is a cable and a half from the mark
       you plotted, and it is the bow that reaches you.
    2. A tow. The tug draws clear beautifully. The barge 200 metres
       astern of her on a wire does not, and at night you may not see it.
    3. Close range. Bearing drift goes as range squared. At two miles a
       degree a minute is comfortable; at four cables the same drift rate
       is a miss of a few yards.

=== lesson C4 ===

  C4  The report
  ----------------

  The report exists so that a man who is not looking at the plot can act on
  it without asking a question. It has a fixed shape for the same reason a
  helm order does.

    "Master 2, bearing 311, Red 20, drawing left, range 14,200,
       CPA 4,100 yards at 18 minutes."

     Master 2     which contact. Numbered so that nobody has to say
                  "the one on the left" with four of them on the left.
     bearing 311  where she is. True, and said in three digits,
                  because that is what goes on the plot and what
                  agrees with the radar.
     Red 20       where to LOOK. Twenty degrees off the port bow.
                  Nobody at the wheel can turn 311 true into a
                  direction to point his face in, and he should not
                  have to. See C7 - and note that this half of the
                  report goes stale the moment you alter course.
     drawing left what she is DOING. A word, not a number, because
                  this is the part the OOD acts on and he must not have
                  to work it out from two bearings in his head.
     range 14,200 how long you have.
     CPA and time how bad it gets, and when.

  Bearing before drift, always. The bearing tells him where to look; the
  drift tells him whether to care. Reversed, he spends the first half of
  the sentence not knowing which contact you mean.

  And say the drift even when there is not any. "Steady" is the most
  important word in the report. Silence about the drift reads as "I have
  not looked", which is the same as "steady" but arrives too late.

  What he does with it: nothing at all, if she is drawing away and the CPA
  is outside night orders. That is most of them, and the discipline is to
  report those the same way and in the same voice as the one that is not.

=== lesson C5 ===

  C5  The arithmetic you do in your head
  ----------------------------------------

  The plot is the truth, but it is always four minutes old. These get you
  close enough to open your mouth now.

    The three-minute rule
    Yards in three minutes = speed in knots times 100.
    12 knots is 1,200 yards in three minutes. That is why marks are taken
    every three minutes: the range change IS the speed, with two noughts.

    The six-minute rule
    Miles in six minutes = speed divided by 10. 15 knots, 1.5 miles.

    Time to CPA
    Range divided by closing rate. Closing rate is the range change per
    minute - read it straight off two marks, no trigonometry.
    8,000 yards closing 600 a minute is thirteen minutes. That is the
    number that decides whether you have time to be polite about it.

    One in sixty, for the CPA itself
    One degree at one mile is about 35 yards. So:

       CPA  =  range in miles  x  degrees she still has to drift  x  35

    She will drift, from now until CPA, at the rate she is drifting now.
    So take the drift per minute, multiply by the minutes to CPA, and that
    is the degrees. Worked: 4 miles, drifting 1.5 degrees a minute, 13
    minutes to run. 1.5 x 13 = 20 degrees. 4 x 20 x 35 = 2,800 yards.

    It is rough. It is meant to be. It tells you which side of the night
    orders you are on before the plot can, and that is the whole job.

=== lesson C6 ===

  C6  Ekelund - range out of a course change
  --------------------------------------------

  On a steady course, bearing rate alone cannot give you range. A slow
  contact close in and a fast one far out produce exactly the same drift,
  and no amount of watching will separate them.

  So you change the geometry yourself. Run a leg, note the bearing rate;
  alter course or speed, settle, run another leg, note it again. Her
  motion across your line of sight has not changed - she is doing what she
  was doing. Every bit of the change in bearing rate is YOURS, and it is
  yours by a known amount. That gives you the range:

    range in kiloyards  =  1.91 x (change in your speed across the
                            line of sight, knots)
                            / (change in bearing rate, deg per min)

    Your speed across the line of sight is your speed times the sine of
    the angle between your course and her bearing. Beam on, all of it;
    dead ahead, none of it.

    The 1.91 is just units: 2,000 yards to the mile over 60 minutes,
    times 57.3 degrees to the radian.

  Worked. She bears 040. You are on 000 at 12 knots and her bearing is
  opening 0.9 degrees a minute. You come round to 090, still 12 knots,
  and it settles at 2.4 a minute.

    across, leg 1 = 12 x sin(000 - 040) = -7.7 knots
    across, leg 2 = 12 x sin(090 - 040) =  9.2 knots
    range = 1.91 x (9.2 - -7.7) / (0.9 - 2.4) = 1.91 x 16.9 / -1.5
          = -21.5 ... take the size: 21,500 yards.

  Three things will ruin it, and they are worth knowing better than the
  formula:
    1. She manoeuvres during the legs. The whole method assumes she did
       not, and it cannot tell you that she did - it just gives you a
       confident wrong answer.
    2. The legs are too short, or you take the rate before the ship has
       settled on the new course.
    3. You barely changed your speed across the line of sight. Then the
       top of the fraction is small, the bottom is smaller, and the noise
       runs the answer. Turn far enough to matter.

  Which is the real lesson of it: a solution you have not manoeuvred
  against is a guess wearing a number.

=== lesson C7 ===

  C7  Relative bearings - so he can find her
  --------------------------------------------

  A true bearing is for the plot. A relative bearing is for the eye.

  Nobody standing at the wheel on a black night can turn 311 degrees
  true into a direction to look in. He would have to remember the
  heading, subtract, and work out which side the answer fell on, while
  steering. Tell him she is fine on the starboard bow and his head is
  already turning.

                            000 RIGHT AHEAD
  
                          ...................
                     ......        |        ......
          Red 45 ....//            |            //..Green 45
               ...    /////        |        /////    ...
              ..          ////     |     ////          ..
             ..               //  ^^^  //               ..
     Red 90  ./////////////////    Y     ////////////////. Green 90
             ..               //       //               ..
              ..          ////           ////          ..
               ...    /////                 /////    ...
           Red 135...//                         //..Green 135
                     ......                 ......
                          ...................
  
                           180 RIGHT ASTERN

  Two ways of saying the number, and you will hear both.

    Red and Green. Red 20 is 20 degrees to port, Green 30 is 30
    to starboard, nought to 180 each side. Royal Navy and British
    practice, and the reason it sticks is that the colours are the
    sidelights: your own red light looks out over your port bow, so
    Red is port. It is unambiguous over a bad intercom because the
    side is a word, not a number that can be misheard.

    Port and starboard. "Port 20", "Starboard 30". The same thing
    in plainer words. United States practice also uses the full
    circle - "bearing 340 relative" - measured clockwise from your
    own bow, which is neater on a plot and worse in the dark.

  And the words, which need no number at all:

      right ahead                       000
      fine on the bow         out to about 35
      broad on the bow                   045
      on the beam                        090
      broad on the quarter               135
      fine on the quarter     in from about 145
      right astern                       180

  Broad on the bow is four points, and four points is 45 degrees.
  Broad on the quarter is four points the other way from astern.
  Everything in the middle is fine, and everything near the beam is
  the beam. The edges are convention, not law - nobody will correct
  you for calling 40 degrees fine rather than broad, and nobody will
  misunderstand you either. That is the point of them.

    Now the trap, and it is a real one.

  A relative bearing changes when SHE moves. It also changes when YOU
  move, by exactly as much, and it cannot tell you which happened.

  Alter twenty degrees to starboard and every contact on the board
  draws twenty degrees left, all at once, with nothing at all having
  changed out in the water. A contact that holds Green 30 through your
  turn has in fact swung twenty degrees - which is the opposite of the
  comfort a steady relative bearing seems to offer.

  So: bearing drift is measured in TRUE bearings, or on a steady
    course, and never across a turn. Everything in C2 and C3
  assumes it. If you take marks either side of an alteration and read
  the drift off them, you will get a confident answer that is wholly
  invented. Take the marks, alter, settle, and start a fresh plot.

  This is also, from the other end, exactly why Ekelund works: the
  change you put in yourself is known, so it can be subtracted out.

  On a yacht it is the same problem in cheaper equipment. A hand
  bearing compass gives you a magnetic bearing you can plot. A pair
  of eyes and a shroud gives you a relative bearing, instantly, in
  the dark, with no instrument at all - and the shroud stays where it
  is only while you hold your course. Steer straight for the two
  minutes you are watching her, or the drift is worthless.

  Which of these this program uses is a setting, because the right
  answer depends entirely on who is listening.  colregs style, or s
  from the contacts menu, and it describes each one before you pick.

  Say both, when it matters: the true bearing so it can be plotted,
  the relative so it can be seen.

    "Master 2, bearing 311, Red 20, drawing left, range 14,200."

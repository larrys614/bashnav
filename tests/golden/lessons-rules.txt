  L1  Rule 5 - Look-out
  -----------------------

  Every vessel shall at all times maintain a proper look-out by sight and
  hearing as well as by all available means appropriate in the prevailing
  circumstances and conditions so as to make a full appraisal of the
  situation and of the risk of collision.

    1. It is the first substantive rule, and it is the one most often
       broken. More collisions are put down to a failure of look-out than
       to any other cause.
    2. 'By sight and hearing' means a person, outside, looking and
       listening - not only a radar screen.
    3. 'All available means' does include radar, AIS and VHF, and if you
       have them and do not use them properly, that is a breach too.
    4. 'At all times' has no exceptions for singlehanders, for tiredness,
       or for the middle of an ocean.

  The look-out must be able to appraise the situation. Someone at the
  wheel who cannot see astern past the sprayhood is not, by themselves, a
  proper look-out.

  Q. Which of these on its own satisfies Rule 5?
       a) a radar with ARPA, watched from the chart table
       b) AIS with a CPA alarm set    c) none of them


  Not quite - the answer is C.  Rule 5 requires sight and hearing as well as all available means. Electronics supplement a look-out; they do not replace one.


  Not quite - the answer is C.  Rule 5 requires sight and hearing as well as all available means. Electronics supplement a look-out; they do not replace one.

=== lesson L2 ===

  L2  Rule 6 - Safe speed
  -------------------------

  Every vessel shall at all times proceed at a safe speed so that she can
  take proper and effective action to avoid collision and be stopped
  within a distance appropriate to the prevailing circumstances.

    1. There is no number in this rule. Safe speed depends on visibility,
       traffic density, manoeuvrability, background lights, the state of
       wind, sea and current, the proximity of hazards and your draught.
    2. If you are using radar there are further factors: its limitations,
       the scale in use, sea and weather clutter, and the possibility that
       small vessels and ice may not be detected at all.
    3. 'Be stopped within a distance appropriate' is the practical test.
       In thick fog with shipping about, that usually means slower than
       feels comfortable.
    4. A sailing vessel is not exempt. Reducing sail is reducing speed.

  Q. What speed does Rule 6 specify in fog?
       a) six knots     b) half your normal speed     c) no number at all


  Not quite - the answer is C.  Rule 6 gives factors, not numbers. The test is that you can stop within a distance appropriate to the circumstances.

=== lesson L3 ===

  L3  Rule 7 - Risk of collision
  --------------------------------

  Every vessel shall use all available means appropriate to determine if
  risk of collision exists. If there is any doubt, such risk shall be
  deemed to exist.

    1. The test is compass bearing. If the compass bearing of an
       approaching vessel does not appreciably change, risk of collision
       exists.
    2. It can exist even with an appreciable bearing change - with a very
       large vessel, a tow, or anything at close range.
    3. Radar must be used properly if fitted and working: long-range
       scanning, and systematic observation of detected objects. A
       glance at the screen is not plotting.
    4. Assumptions shall not be made on the basis of scanty information,
       especially scanty radar information. One AIS target is not a
       picture of the traffic.

  Take the bearing with a hand bearing compass over several minutes, or
  line her up against a stanchion and keep your head still. Steady
  bearing, decreasing range: you are going to hit her.

  Q. A ship's compass bearing is steady and the range is closing. What does Rule 7 say?
       a) risk of collision exists    b) risk exists only inside two miles
       c) no risk while she is more than 30 degrees off the bow


  Correct.  A steady compass bearing with decreasing range is the definition of risk of collision. And if in any doubt, risk shall be deemed to exist.


  Not quite - the answer is A.  A steady compass bearing with decreasing range is the definition of risk of collision. And if in any doubt, risk shall be deemed to exist.

=== lesson L4 ===

  L4  Rule 8 - Action to avoid collision
  ----------------------------------------

  Any action shall be positive, made in ample time and with due regard to
  good seamanship.

    1. Alterations of course or speed shall be large enough to be readily
       apparent to another vessel observing visually or by radar. A
       succession of small alterations is exactly what the rule forbids.
    2. If there is sea room, an alteration of course alone may be the most
       effective action - provided it is made in good time, is substantial,
       and does not result in another close-quarters situation.
    3. Action to avoid collision shall result in passing at a safe
       distance, and its effectiveness shall be carefully checked until
       the other vessel is finally past and clear.
    4. If necessary to avoid collision or allow more time to assess, slack
       off, stop, or reverse. Taking all way off is always available to you.

  A ten degree alteration at four miles is invisible on the other bridge.
  Thirty degrees, early, is a signal in itself.

  Q. Which action best meets Rule 8?
       a) five degrees now, and five more if she does not respond
       b) a single alteration of 30 degrees, made early
       c) hold on until one mile, then alter hard


  Not quite - the answer is B.  Action must be positive, made in ample time, and large enough to be readily apparent to the other vessel visually or by radar.


  Correct.  Action must be positive, made in ample time, and large enough to be readily apparent to the other vessel visually or by radar.

=== lesson L5 ===

  L5  Rule 9 - Narrow channels
  ------------------------------

  A vessel proceeding along the course of a narrow channel or fairway
  shall keep as near to the outer limit which lies on her starboard side
  as is safe and practicable.

    1. Keep to the starboard side of the channel. That is the whole rule
       in one line.
    2. A vessel of less than 20 metres, or a sailing vessel, shall not
       impede the passage of a vessel which can safely navigate only
       within the channel. Nor shall a vessel engaged in fishing.
    3. Do not cross a channel if it impedes a vessel that can only
       navigate within it. If in doubt about her intentions, the doubt
       signal - five or more short blasts - is available.
    4. Overtaking in a narrow channel needs the other vessel's agreement,
       signalled by sound: two prolonged and one short means you intend to
       pass on her starboard side.

  'Shall not impede' is not the same as give way. It means you must keep
  clear early enough that the question of who gives way never arises.

  Q. In a narrow channel, which side do you keep to?
       a) the port side     b) the starboard side     c) the deepest water


  Not quite - the answer is B.  As near to the outer limit on your starboard side as is safe and practicable.


  Correct.  As near to the outer limit on your starboard side as is safe and practicable.


  Not quite - the answer is B.  As near to the outer limit on your starboard side as is safe and practicable.


  Not quite - the answer is B.  As near to the outer limit on your starboard side as is safe and practicable.

=== lesson L6 ===

  L6  Rule 10 - Traffic separation schemes
  ------------------------------------------

  A vessel using a traffic separation scheme shall proceed in the
  appropriate lane in the general direction of traffic flow, keep clear of
  the separation line or zone, and normally join or leave at the ends.

    1. If you must join at the side, do so at as small an angle to the
       general direction of flow as practicable.
    2. If you must cross, cross on a heading as nearly as practicable at
       right angles to the direction of flow. Heading, not track - so you
       do not crab across with the tide to make a right-angled ground
       track. This was changed deliberately: a beam-on aspect is what makes
       you visible and predictable.
    3. A vessel under 20 metres, a sailing vessel, or a vessel fishing
       shall not impede the safe passage of a power-driven vessel
       following a lane.
    4. Inshore traffic zones are not for through traffic, with limited
       exceptions - vessels under 20 metres, sailing vessels, fishing
       vessels, and vessels going to or from a place inside the zone.

  Q. Crossing a traffic separation scheme, what should be at right angles to the flow?
       a) your heading     b) your ground track     c) your wake


  Correct.  Rule 10(c) says heading. Do not crab across with the tide to make the track square - a beam-on aspect is what makes you visible.


  Not quite - the answer is A.  Rule 10(c) says heading. Do not crab across with the tide to make the track square - a beam-on aspect is what makes you visible.

=== lesson L7 ===

  L7  Rule 12 - Sailing vessels
  -------------------------------

  When two sailing vessels are approaching one another so as to involve
  risk of collision:

    1. When each has the wind on a different side, the vessel with the
       wind on her port side shall keep out of the way of the other.
       Port tack gives way to starboard tack.
    2. When both have the wind on the same side, the vessel to windward
       shall keep out of the way of the vessel to leeward. Windward gives
       way - which surprises most people the first time.
    3. If a vessel with the wind on the port side sees a vessel to
       windward and cannot tell whether that vessel has the wind on the
       port or the starboard side, she shall keep out of the way.
    4. The windward side is the side opposite that on which the mainsail
       is carried - or, for a square-rigged vessel, the largest fore-and-
       aft sail.

  Note that this rule applies between two sailing vessels only. Rule 18
  governs a sailing vessel meeting anything else.

  Q. Two sailing vessels, both with the wind on the starboard side. Who gives way?
       a) the vessel to windward     b) the vessel to leeward     c) the overtaking vessel


  Correct.  Same tack: windward keeps out of the way of leeward.


  Not quite - the answer is A.  Same tack: windward keeps out of the way of leeward.


  Not quite - the answer is A.  Same tack: windward keeps out of the way of leeward.

=== lesson L8 ===

  L8  Rule 13 - Overtaking
  --------------------------

  Any vessel overtaking any other shall keep out of the way of the vessel
  being overtaken. This rule overrides everything in the other steering
  rules - it applies notwithstanding anything in Rules 4 to 18.

    1. A vessel is overtaking when coming up on another from a direction
       more than 22.5 degrees abaft her beam.
    2. The practical test at night: if you can see only her sternlight,
       and neither of her sidelights, you are overtaking.
    3. When in any doubt whether you are overtaking, assume that you are
       and act accordingly.
    4. Any subsequent alteration of the bearing between the two shall not
       make you a crossing vessel or relieve you of the duty to keep clear
       until you are finally past and clear.

  A sailing vessel overtaking a power-driven vessel keeps clear. This is
  the case that most often catches sailors out.

  Q. At night you can see another vessel's sternlight and neither sidelight. What are you?
       a) crossing     b) overtaking     c) head-on


  Not quite - the answer is B.  Sternlight only means you are more than 22.5 degrees abaft her beam: you are overtaking, and you keep clear until finally past and clear.


  Correct.  Sternlight only means you are more than 22.5 degrees abaft her beam: you are overtaking, and you keep clear until finally past and clear.

  L9  Rule 14 - Head-on situation
  ---------------------------------

  When two power-driven vessels are meeting on reciprocal or nearly
  reciprocal courses so as to involve risk of collision, each shall alter
  her course to starboard so that each shall pass on the port side of the
  other.

    1. Neither vessel is the stand-on vessel. Both act.
    2. The situation shall be deemed to exist when a vessel sees the other
       ahead or nearly ahead - by night, both sidelights, or the masthead
       lights in a line or nearly in a line.
    3. When in doubt whether such a situation exists, assume that it does
       and act accordingly.
    4. This rule is between power-driven vessels only.

  Alter to starboard, early and boldly, and pass port to port: red to red.

  Q. Two power-driven vessels meeting head-on. Who is the stand-on vessel?
       a) the larger     b) the one to starboard     c) neither


  Not quite - the answer is C.  In a head-on situation there is no stand-on vessel. Each alters to starboard and they pass port to port.


  Not quite - the answer is C.  In a head-on situation there is no stand-on vessel. Each alters to starboard and they pass port to port.

  L10  Rules 15 and 16 - Crossing, and the give-way vessel
  ---------------------------------------------------------

  When two power-driven vessels are crossing so as to involve risk of
  collision, the vessel which has the other on her own starboard side
  shall keep out of the way and shall, if the circumstances of the case
  admit, avoid crossing ahead of the other vessel.

    1. Starboard side, so at night you see her red sidelight. Red means
       stop: she is on your right and you give way.
    2. Avoid crossing ahead. In practice, alter to starboard and pass
       under her stern: it is the action that is unmistakable from her
       bridge.
    3. Rule 16 tells the give-way vessel to take early and substantial
       action to keep well clear. Early and substantial.
    4. This rule is between power-driven vessels. A crossing sailing
       vessel is a Rule 18 problem.

  Q. You are power-driven and see another power-driven vessel's red sidelight, crossing. What do you do?
       a) stand on     b) give way, and avoid crossing ahead of her
       c) give way by altering to port


  Not quite - the answer is B.  Her red light means she is on your starboard side. You give way, and Rule 15 says avoid crossing ahead - so alter to starboard and pass under her stern.


  Correct.  Her red light means she is on your starboard side. You give way, and Rule 15 says avoid crossing ahead - so alter to starboard and pass under her stern.

=== lesson L11 ===

  L11  Rule 17 - Action by the stand-on vessel
  ---------------------------------------------

  Where one vessel is to keep out of the way, the other shall keep her
  course and speed. But standing on is not a licence to do nothing.

    1. Normally: keep your course and speed, so that the give-way vessel
       can work out a solution around you.
    2. You MAY take action as soon as it becomes apparent to you that she
       is not taking appropriate action. This is permission, not duty.
    3. You SHALL take such action as will best aid to avoid collision when
       you find yourself so close that collision cannot be avoided by the
       give-way vessel's action alone. This is a duty.
    4. If you are a power-driven vessel taking action in a crossing
       situation, do not alter to port for a vessel on your own port side.

  The give-way vessel's obligation does not go away because you have
  acted. Both of you remain bound to avoid collision.

  Q. You are the stand-on vessel and she is doing nothing about it. What does Rule 17 allow?
       a) nothing until collision is unavoidable    b) you may act as soon as it is
          apparent she is not acting    c) you must alter to port


  Not quite - the answer is B.  Rule 17(a)(ii) permits you to act as soon as it becomes apparent she is not taking appropriate action, and 17(b) requires you to act when collision cannot be avoided by her action alone.


  Correct.  Rule 17(a)(ii) permits you to act as soon as it becomes apparent she is not taking appropriate action, and 17(b) requires you to act when collision cannot be avoided by her action alone.

  L12  Rule 18 - Responsibilities between vessels
  ------------------------------------------------

  Except where Rules 9, 10 and 13 otherwise require, the pecking order
  runs from the least manoeuvrable to the most:

       not under command
       restricted in her ability to manoeuvre
       engaged in fishing
       sailing
       power-driven

    1. Each keeps out of the way of everything above it in that list.
    2. A vessel constrained by her draught sits slightly outside the list:
       Rule 18(d) says every vessel shall avoid impeding her safe passage,
       and she must navigate with particular caution.
    3. A seaplane on the water keeps well clear of all vessels; a
       wing-in-ground craft, when taking off, landing or in flight near
       the surface, keeps well clear of all vessels.
    4. The exceptions matter: in a narrow channel, in a traffic scheme,
       and when overtaking, the ordinary order can be reversed.

  Sailing vessels are third from the bottom, not at the top. A yacht keeps
  out of the way of a fishing vessel, and out of the way of anything not
  under command.

  Q. A yacht under sail meets a vessel engaged in fishing. Who keeps clear?
       a) the fishing vessel     b) the yacht     c) neither, it is a crossing situation


  Not quite - the answer is B.  Rule 18: a sailing vessel keeps out of the way of a vessel engaged in fishing, and of one restricted in her ability to manoeuvre, and of one not under command.


  Correct.  Rule 18: a sailing vessel keeps out of the way of a vessel engaged in fishing, and of one restricted in her ability to manoeuvre, and of one not under command.

  Not quite - the answer is B.  Rule 18: a sailing vessel keeps out of the way of a vessel engaged in fishing, and of one restricted in her ability to manoeuvre, and of one not under command.

=== lesson L13 ===

  L13  Rule 19 - Restricted visibility
  -------------------------------------

  This rule applies to vessels not in sight of one another when navigating
  in or near an area of restricted visibility. It is a different world
  from Rules 11 to 18.

    1. There is no stand-on vessel. Nobody has right of way. The give-way
       and stand-on rules simply do not apply.
    2. Every vessel shall proceed at a safe speed adapted to the
       circumstances, with engines ready for immediate manoeuvre.
    3. A vessel which detects by radar alone another vessel shall
       determine if a close-quarters situation is developing, and if so
       take avoiding action in ample time. If that action is an alteration
       of course, avoid: an alteration to port for a vessel forward of the
       beam other than for a vessel being overtaken, and an alteration
       towards a vessel abeam or abaft the beam.
    4. A vessel which hears apparently forward of her beam the fog signal
       of another vessel, or which cannot avoid a close-quarters situation
       with a vessel forward of her beam, shall reduce to the minimum at
       which she can be kept on course - and if necessary take all her way
       off, and in any event navigate with extreme caution until the
       danger of collision is over.

  Q. In fog, you hear one prolonged and two short forward of the beam. Who is the stand-on vessel?
       a) you     b) she is     c) neither - Rule 19 has no stand-on vessel


  Not quite - the answer is C.  Rule 19 applies to vessels not in sight of one another, and it has no give-way and stand-on. Reduce to the minimum steerage way and, if necessary, take all way off.


  Not quite - the answer is C.  Rule 19 applies to vessels not in sight of one another, and it has no give-way and stand-on. Reduce to the minimum steerage way and, if necessary, take all way off.

  L14  Rules 20 to 31 - Lights and shapes
  ----------------------------------------

  Lights are shown from sunset to sunrise, and in restricted visibility,
  and may be shown at any other time when it is considered necessary.
  Shapes are shown by day.

    1. The arcs are fixed and worth knowing by heart: masthead light 225
       degrees, sidelights 112.5 each, sternlight 135. Sidelights plus
       sternlight make the full circle.
    2. What you see tells you her aspect. Both sidelights: she is nearly
       bow-on. One sidelight and a masthead light: she is crossing. Only a
       white light: she may be a sternlight, an anchor light, or a small
       boat - keep watching until you know which.
    3. All-round lights in a vertical line are the vessel telling you what
       she is doing and what she cannot do. Red over white, fishing at
       night. Red over red, the captain is dead - not under command.
       Red, white, red - restricted in her ability to manoeuvre.
    4. Day shapes carry the same meanings: a ball for anchored, three
       balls aground, two balls not under command, ball-diamond-ball
       restricted, a cone point down for a sailing vessel under power.

  Q. What arc does a masthead light cover?
       a) 112.5 degrees     b) 225 degrees     c) 360 degrees


  Not quite - the answer is B.  225 degrees, from right ahead to 22.5 degrees abaft the beam on each side. The sidelights cover 112.5 each and the sternlight the remaining 135.


  Correct.  225 degrees, from right ahead to 22.5 degrees abaft the beam on each side. The sidelights cover 112.5 each and the sternlight the remaining 135.


  Not quite - the answer is B.  225 degrees, from right ahead to 22.5 degrees abaft the beam on each side. The sidelights cover 112.5 each and the sternlight the remaining 135.

  L15  Rules 32 to 37 - Sound and light signals
  ----------------------------------------------

  Sound signals fall into two groups, and the difference matters more
  than the signals themselves.

    1. Rule 34 signals are for vessels IN SIGHT of one another. They say
       what you are doing right now: one short, I am altering to
       starboard; two short, to port; three short, my engines are going
       astern.
    2. Rule 35 signals are for RESTRICTED VISIBILITY, when you cannot see
       each other. They say what kind of vessel you are: one prolonged
       for a power-driven vessel making way; one prolonged and two short
       for anything from not-under-command to a sailing vessel.
    3. Five or more short and rapid blasts is the doubt signal, and the
       most under-used signal at sea. It means: I do not understand what
       you are doing, or I do not think you are doing enough.
    4. A prolonged blast is four to six seconds. A short blast is about
       one second. Vessels of 100 metres or more carry a bell and a gong;
       under 12 metres you need not carry the equipment, but you must make
       some other efficient sound signal.

  Q. What does one prolonged blast mean from a vessel you cannot see, in fog?
       a) I am altering to starboard     b) a power-driven vessel making way
       c) I am approaching a bend


  Not quite - the answer is B.  In restricted visibility (Rule 35) one prolonged every two minutes is a power-driven vessel making way through the water. The same signal in sight of another vessel, at a bend, is Rule 34(e).


  Correct.  In restricted visibility (Rule 35) one prolonged every two minutes is a power-driven vessel making way through the water. The same signal in sight of another vessel, at a bend, is Rule 34(e).

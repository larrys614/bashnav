=== weather what ===

  WHAT THE LOG SAYS
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Pressure 1003.0, -7.1 hPa in 12.0 hours.
  Allowing for the daily atmospheric tide: -7.1 hPa.
     the tide alone accounts for -0.0 of that. It is semidiurnal,
     about 1.4 hPa in the tropics and much less in high latitudes,
     and in the tropics it falls 2-3 mb every afternoon in fine weather.
  A definite fall. Something is coming.
     a rate matters more than a level: 1000 hPa steady is a quiet day,
     1015 falling fast is not.

  Wind has BACKED 20 degrees, 170 to 150.
     northern hemisphere: backing with a falling glass puts the centre
     to your north and closing. This is the one to take seriously.

  Back to the wind: the low is roughly 240 from you.
     Buys Ballot. Stand with your back to the wind: in the northern
     hemisphere the low is on your left, in the southern, on your right.

  The cloud has run the whole warm-front sequence: high, then
  middle, and now low stratus. The front is on you or past.
     cirrus -> cirrostratus -> altostratus -> nimbostratus and rain
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  This reasons only from what you logged. It is not a forecast:
  no model, no chart, no GRIB. It is you, reading your own barometer.

=== weather learn fluid ===

  AIR IS A FLUID, AND MOIST AIR IS LIGHTER
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Air is a fluid. At the speeds anything on a boat moves it barely
  compresses, so it behaves very much like water - and the weather is
  fluid dynamics happening to a very large, very thin bath.

  And here is the counterintuitive one:

    Warm MOIST air is LIGHTER than warm DRY air.

  Humid air feels heavy. It is not. At the same temperature and pressure
  a given volume holds a fixed NUMBER of molecules - so water vapour does
  not add molecules to the box, it replaces them:

         H2O  18      pushing out      N2  28
                                       O2  32

  Every water molecule that goes in evicts something heavier. Same count,
  less mass, lower density.

  This is the engine of everything else. Warm moist air is buoyant
  twice over - once for being warm, once for being wet - which is why the
  tropics convect so violently, and why a thunderstorm is a heat engine
  rather than a wind event.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: It is 30 C and very humid. Is that air heavier or lighter than 30 C and dry?

Lighter. Water molecules at 18 replace nitrogen at 28 and oxygen at 32, and a given volume holds a fixed number of molecules. Warm and wet is buoyant twice over, which is what builds thunderstorms.
=== weather learn data ===

  THE THREE KINDS OF WEATHER DATA
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Most sailors never learn this, and it decides how much to believe.

  OBSERVATION   an instrument reading of what is happening now.
                 Not a forecast - but it is true.

  ANALYSIS      a chart drawn from observations and then edited and
                 verified by a human forecaster. A surface analysis,
                 most national weather service products. The best
                 available picture of NOW.

  MODEL OUTPUT  a computer's projection. What sailors call a GRIB.
                 Nobody has looked at it.

  The trap is the third. A GRIB arrives in colour, at high resolution, out
  to seven days, looking authoritative - and it is a machine's
  extrapolation with no human in the loop. Sailors treat it as gospel.
  The rule: check you are looking at an analysis, not a model run.

  And it places this tool honestly. It works entirely in the first
  column. Every number in it is something you measured. It cannot
  forecast - but what it reasons FROM is the one category that is never
  wrong, and the only one still there when the antenna comes down.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: A seven-day GRIB and a 24-hour surface analysis disagree. Which do you believe?

The analysis. A human forecaster has looked at it and corrected it. The GRIB is a machine's extrapolation that nobody has seen, and the further out it runs the less it is worth.
=== weather learn tide ===

  THE ATMOSPHERIC TIDE
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  The sun heats the air, the earth turns under it, and a pressure wave
  travels westward with the sun. It is on your barometer, every day.

  The odd part: the dominant component is SEMIdiurnal - twice a day,
  not once. Why once-a-day heating produces a twice-a-day pressure signal
  is a genuine puzzle in meteorology.

    amplitude in the tropics     about 1.4 hPa
    maxima                       about 1000 and 2200 local
    minima                       about 0400 and 1600 local
    and it falls off steeply    ~1.2 hPa at the equator, ~0.2 at 45 deg

  Which is a trap worth knowing about. In the tropics the glass
  falls two to three millibars between mid-morning and mid-afternoon
  every single day, in perfect weather. A sailor who does not know
  that reads a routine afternoon fall as a system approaching.

  This app corrects for it before saying anything about tendency, and
  tells you how much of the change was the tide. Give it your longitude
  - it needs local solar time to know where in the cycle you are.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: You are at 12N. The glass has fallen 2 hPa between 1000 and 1600. Worry?

Probably not - that is very close to the daily atmospheric tide, which is about 1.4 hPa of semidiurnal swing in the tropics with a minimum near 1600. Correct for it before reading anything into it. At 50N the same fall would mean much more.
=== weather learn coriolis ===

  CORIOLIS, WHICH IS NOT A FORCE
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  The earth turns underneath you. Describe anything's motion relative to
  the ground and it appears to curve. The "force" is the bookkeeping
  entry for having chosen a rotating frame - nothing is pushing.

  Its size goes as sin(latitude):

    at the poles     full strength
    at 45 degrees    about 0.7 of it
    at the equator   ZERO

  Two things it buys you.

  First: no tropical cyclone forms within about 5 degrees of the
  equator, because there is nothing there to spin it up. All that heat
  and moisture and no rotation.

  Second: it is half of Buys Ballot, which is the most useful rule this
  app has. Stand with your back to the wind and the low is on your left
  in the northern hemisphere - and on your right in the southern, because
  the sense of the rotation reverses.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Why does no hurricane ever form on the equator?

Coriolis goes as sin(latitude) and is zero there. All the heat and moisture in the world, and nothing to start it rotating. About 5 degrees is the practical minimum.
=== weather learn gradient ===

  GRADIENT WIND, SURFACE WIND, AND FRICTION
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Well above the sea the wind balances the pressure gradient against
  Coriolis and runs along the isobars. That is the gradient wind, and
  it is what a weather chart draws.

  Down where you are, friction slows the air. Slower air feels less
  Coriolis. So pressure wins a little, and the surface wind blows
  across the isobars, toward the low:

    over water   backed 10-20 degrees, and about 2/3 of gradient speed
    over land    backed 30-40 degrees, and slower still
    southern hemisphere: veered, not backed

  This is the chart-to-deck translation - what the isobars mean for
  what you will actually get on the wind instrument.

  And the same boundary layer does something else you already know about.
  Friction slows the lowest air, so wind speed climbs with height, and
  the apparent wind aloft is stronger and further aft than at the boom.
  That is why a sail is twisted. One piece of physics, two apps.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: The chart shows a 30 knot gradient wind. What do you expect on deck, offshore, in the northern hemisphere?

Around 20 knots - roughly two thirds - and backed 10 to 20 degrees from the isobar direction, because friction slows the surface air, which weakens Coriolis, so pressure gradient wins a little and the wind blows across the isobars toward the low.
=== weather learn lapse ===

  LAPSE RATES, STABILITY, AND THE CLOUD BASE
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Air cools as it rises, because it expands. How fast is the whole game.

    dry adiabatic     about 3.0 C per 1000 ft   an unsaturated parcel
    saturated         about 1.5 C per 1000 ft   condensing gives heat back
    environmental     whatever the air actually does - measured

  Compare the environmental rate with the adiabatic and you have
  stability. If the surrounding air cools FASTER with height than the
  parcel does, the parcel stays warmer than its surroundings, keeps
  rising, and you get towering cloud. That is instability, and it is why
  a saturated parcel can keep going where a dry one would have stopped.

  THE CLOUD BASE falls straight out of it, and the arithmetic is
  something you can do on deck:

    a rising parcel cools at         3.0 C / 1000 ft
    its dew point falls at           0.5 C / 1000 ft
    so the spread closes at          2.5 C / 1000 ft

    height (ft) = (air temp - dew point, in C) x 400

  400, not 333. It is not the lapse rate, it is the DIFFERENCE between
  two of them. Divide by the lapse rate alone and you put the base a
  fifth too low. And it is air temperature, not sea temperature - the
  sea is a fair proxy offshore and badly wrong near a front.

  A field of cumulus with flat bases all at one height IS that level made
  visible. Above it, the trade inversion caps the marine layer, and the
  flat tops are you looking at the underside of the lid.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Air 18 C, dew point 12 C. How high is the cloud base?

About 2,400 ft: a spread of 6 C, and the spread closes at 2.5 C per 1000 ft, so 6 x 400. Not 6 x 333 - the divisor is the DIFFERENCE between the parcel's lapse rate and the dew point's, not the lapse rate itself.
=== weather learn cells ===

  THE HEAT ENGINE, AND THE THREE CELLS
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  The tropics take in more energy from the sun than they radiate away.
  The poles do the reverse. The atmosphere and the ocean exist, as far as
  weather is concerned, to move the difference poleward.

  Most of the atmosphere's share travels as latent heat - water
  evaporated in the tropics and released as rain somewhere else. The
  water cycle is not a side effect of the weather. It is the working
  fluid.

    Hadley    rises at the equator, descends near 30 degrees
    Ferrel    30 to 60, and the one you probably sail in
    Polar     60 to the pole

  The air descending at 30 degrees has already rained out everything it
  had, so it arrives warm and dry. That gives you the subtropical highs,
  the horse latitudes, the trade winds on their equatorward side - and
  most of the world's deserts sit at that latitude for this reason.

  And it closes the loop with the last lesson: that same descending
  warm air is the inversion capping the marine layer. The lid over your
  flat-topped cumulus is the far end of a circulation that started as
  thunderstorms on the equator.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Why are so many of the world's deserts near 30 degrees latitude?

That is where the Hadley cell descends. The air rose at the equator, rained out everything it had, and comes back down warm and dry. The subtropical highs, the horse latitudes and the deserts are all the same descending branch.
=== weather learn h500 ===

  THE 500 MILLIBAR CHART, AND THE 564 LINE
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  A 500 mb chart is a topographic map of a pressure surface: the
  contours are the HEIGHT at which the pressure is 500 millibars, drawn
  in decametres. So "564" means the 5,640 metre contour. Low heights are
  cold air, high heights are warm.

  It is around 18,000 feet, with about half the atmosphere's mass above
  and half below - which is why its flow is a fair proxy for where a
  whole system gets carried. The steering level.

  Five rules a mariner can use from one chart:

    1. The surface storm track lies 300 to 600 nm poleward of the
       5640 m contour, and runs parallel to it.

    2. All the gale force winds are poleward of the 564 line.
       Stay on its equatorward side and you stay out of the gales. This
       is the routing rule, and it is why the standard book on the
       subject is called Heavy Weather Avoidance.

    3. Surface lows and fronts move at a third to a half of the
       500 mb wind speed above them.

    4. Surface wind behind the system, in the cold air to the west
       and southwest, is about half the 500 mb wind speed.

    5. Tighter contours mean stronger wind - the upper-air version of
       close isobars. And the 588 contour marks the subtropical ridge,
       which is what steers tropical systems.

  You do not need the internet for this. A 500 mb chart comes in by HF
  radiofax, on an SSB receiver, at sea, with no connectivity at all. Read
  three numbers off it and weather chart will walk the rules with you.

  From the Mariner's Guide to the 500-Millibar Chart, by Joe Sienkiewicz
  of NOAA's Ocean Prediction Center and Lee Chesneau - US Navy, then OPC,
  who spent his retirement teaching this to sailors. See docs/SOURCES.md.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: The 564 line runs east-west 200 nm north of you. Where is the storm track, and are you in the gales?

The track is 300 to 600 nm poleward of the 564 line and parallel to it, so well north of you. And all the gale force winds are poleward of that line - so at 200 nm south of it you are on the right side of it. That is the routing rule.
=== weather learn cyclone ===

  HOW A TROPICAL CYCLONE IS MADE, AND HOW TO KEEP AWAY
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  This is the payoff for the other lessons: it needs all of them.

    warm deep water    about 26.5 C, and 50 m deep, or the storm
                       stirs up cold water and kills itself
    enough Coriolis    so not within ~5 degrees of the equator
    a moist unstable column  moist air is buoyant twice over
    weak wind shear    or the tower is torn apart before it organises
    something to start it  a wave, a trough, an old front

  Take any one away and you get a lot of rain and no cyclone. Which is
  useful: it tells you which conditions to worry about.

  AND AVOIDANCE IS A PROBLEM YOU ALREADY KNOW.

  A storm centre has a position, a course and a speed. So do you. You
  want the largest possible closest point of approach. That is exactly
  the relative-motion problem from colregs - the same vector triangle,
  the same bearing drift, the same answer. The dangerous semicircle is
  just the half where the storm's own motion adds to its wind.

  Which is why the tools in this suite keep turning out to be the same
  few ideas wearing different hats.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Sea surface 27 C, moist unstable air, a nice tropical wave - and 40 knots of shear aloft. Cyclone?

No. Shear tears the tower apart before it can organise. Four of the five ingredients is not a storm, which is exactly why knowing the list is useful.
=== weather learn seasons ===

  THE TILT, THE SEASONS, AND AN HONEST BOUNDARY
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  The earth's axis leans 23.5 degrees and keeps pointing the same way
  as it goes round. So each hemisphere leans toward the sun for half the
  year. That is the whole of the seasons - not distance from the sun,
  which barely varies and is at its smallest in northern winter.

  What it buys you at sea: the whole belt of weather migrates north and
  south with it. The equatorial convergence, the subtropical ridge, the
  storm tracks. A cyclone season is a season because the tilt makes it
  one - warm water and a ridge in the right place at the same time.

  Now the honest part.

  The axis also wobbles, a full circle every 26,000 years, and the orbit
  stretches and relaxes on cycles of 100,000. Those are real and they
  drive ice ages.

  They are climate, not weather, and nothing on your passage depends
  on them. They are in this lesson because they are interesting and
  because you asked, and they are labelled so you know which is which.

  Every other lesson here earns its place by changing what you would do.
  This half of this one does not, and pretending otherwise would be the
  beginning of a tool full of things that sound like knowledge.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Northern winter is when the earth is CLOSEST to the sun. So why is it cold?

Because the seasons are the 23.5 degree tilt, not the distance. The northern hemisphere is leaning away, so the sun is low, the days are short, and the same energy is spread over more ground. The distance varies by about 3 percent and is swamped by it.

=== lesson P1 ===

  P1  Dead reckoning: course, speed and time
  --------------------------------------------

  Everything else in this program needs a position to start from. A
  sight is reduced from an assumed position; a running fix is advanced
  along a course and a speed; even deciding which of two circle
  crossings is you needs a rough idea of where you are.

  Dead reckoning is that idea, and it is the only position you can
  always have. No sky, no satellites, no landmarks: just what you
  steered, how fast, and for how long.

  It is arithmetic and makes no claim to be true. That is not a
  weakness as long as you remember it.

  THE METHOD

    1. Distance run = speed x time. Nothing subtle.
    2. Resolve it onto north and east:

         northing = distance x cos(course)
         departure = distance x sin(course)

    3. Northing IS difference of latitude: one minute of latitude is
       one nautical mile, everywhere on earth. That is what a mile is.

    4. Departure is NOT difference of longitude. The meridians close
       together toward the poles, so a mile of easting is worth more
       than a minute of longitude:

         difference of longitude = departure / cos(latitude)

  At 50 degrees north, cos 50 is 0.64 - so a mile east is 1.56
  minutes of longitude. At 70 north it is nearly three. Forget this
  and your DR walks east or west of the truth, and the error grows
  with latitude, which is exactly where the weather is worst.
  A worked run, computed by this lesson as you read it:

      course 050 T    speed 6.5 kn    time 90 min

                     N
                     |
                     |      /
            northing |    /   distance 9.75 nm
             6.27    |  /
                     |/ 50 deg
                     +------------ E
                     departure 7.47 nm

      difference of latitude      6.27'  = 6.27 nm
      departure                   7.47 nm
      cos(latitude 50)             0.641
      difference of longitude    11.66'

  Note the last three lines. The departure and the difference of
  longitude are different numbers for the same movement east.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. You steer 050 T at 6.5 knots for 90 minutes. How far north and how
     far east are you?
       a) 6.3 nm north and 7.5 nm east
       b) 9.8 nm north and 9.8 nm east
       c) 7.5 nm north and 6.3 nm east

=== answer P1 (given b) ===

  Not quite - the answer is A.  9.75 nm run; northing is distance x cos 50 = 6.3, departure is x sin 50 = 7.5. C has the sine and cosine swapped, which is the mistake to know about.

=== lesson P2 ===

  P2  Three kinds of position, and which to believe
  ---------------------------------------------------

  A chart can carry three marks that all claim to be where you are.
  They are not the same claim.

    DR   DEAD RECKONING. Course, speed and time from the last known
         position. No observation in it at all. It is where you would
         be if the water had stood still and the helm had been
         perfect.

    EP   ESTIMATED POSITION. The DR with what you know about the
         water put back in - set, drift, leeway. Better than the DR,
         and still an assumption rather than an observation.

    FIX  Two or more lines of position, each from something outside
         the ship. The only one of the three with an observation in
         it.

  The order of confidence never changes: fix, then EP, then DR. A
  navigator who cannot say which of the three a mark is has lost the
  only thing that matters about it.

  WHY THIS MODULE COMES BEFORE THE SIGHTS

  A celestial fix is not independent of your DR. Three places it
  leans on it:

    1. The assumed position comes from the DR. Put it far from the
       truth and the intercept gets long, and a long intercept is
       where the straight-line approximation starts to cost you.
    2. A running fix advances the earlier line of position along your
       course and speed. A log reading 5 per cent high puts that error
       straight into the fix - and it will look like a good fix.
    3. With one body you have a line, not a position. Which point on
       that line you are is a DR question.

  So the fix is only ever as good as the reckoning you ran it from.
  That is the honest version of "celestial navigation": it is
  reckoning, corrected by observation, not observation alone.

  ONE SHIP, THREE MARKS, FOUR HOURS APART

  
  
  
  
       o 0400 fix              x 0800 DR
  
  
                   0800 fix O
                               e 0800 EP
  
  
  
  
  o = where you were   x = DR   e = EP   O = where you actually are
  one row and one column = 1 nm.

  The DR ran 24 miles east on course and log alone. The EP allowed
  four miles of south-going set. The fix landed near the EP and not
  on it, which is the normal case - and the reason the next set and
  drift is worked from the fix and never from the estimate.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. Which of the three would you bet the ship on, and why?
       a) the DR, because it is arithmetic and cannot be wrong
       b) the fix, because it is the only one with an observation in it
       c) the EP, because it allows for the current

=== answer P2 (given b) ===

  Correct.  The DR's arithmetic being right is not the question - it is right about an assumption. The EP adds a better assumption. Only the fix looked outside the ship.

=== lesson P3 ===

  P3  Set and drift: what the sea did while you were not looking
  ----------------------------------------------------------------

  You have a DR for 0800 and a star fix for 0800, and they are not in
  the same place. The difference has a name and a use.

    SET    the direction the water is setting TOWARD, in degrees true
    DRIFT  its rate, in knots

  Take the bearing and distance from the DR to the fix. The bearing
  is the set. The distance divided by the hours since the last fix is
  the drift. That is the whole calculation.

  TWO THINGS TO BE HONEST ABOUT

    1. It is a remainder, not a measurement. Nothing in it was
       observed. It is everything your reckoning failed to account
       for, and the current is only one item on that list: leeway, a
       log reading high or low, steering that averaged a few degrees
       off the ordered course, and the error of the fix itself are all
       inside the same number.
    2. Direction is the commonest mistake. The set is FROM the DR
       TOWARD the fix. If your reckoning put you west of where you
       really were, the water has been setting you east - toward the
       fix, not away from it. The reciprocal is a plausible, wrong,
       and completely silent error.

  IN CELNAV

    celnav current   given the DR, the fix and the hours, the set and
                     the drift
    celnav ep        given a position, a course, a speed and a time,
                     the DR - and with a set and drift, the EP

  Carry a set and drift forward as the best estimate you have, and
  expect the next fix to revise it. A tidal stream turns; an ocean
  current does not, much. Which you are in decides how much faith the
  number deserves tomorrow.
  Worked as you read it, by the same code the command uses:

      0800 DR               50 00.0'N  005 00.0'W
      0800 star fix         50 02.8'N  004 55.6'W
      hours since the DR    4

      the fix lies          4.0 nm  045 T  from the DR
      SET                   045 T
      DRIFT                 1.00 kn

  Reverse the two positions and the set comes out 225 T instead of
  045 T - the same magnitude, the opposite conclusion, and nothing on
  the page to tell you which one you did.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. Your 0800 DR and your 0800 star fix are 4 miles apart, the fix to the
     north-east. The last fix was at 0400. What is the set and drift?
       a) set 045 T, drift 1.0 kn
       b) set 225 T, drift 1.0 kn
       c) set 045 T, drift 4.0 kn

=== answer P3 (given b) ===

  Not quite - the answer is A.  Set is FROM the DR TOWARD the fix, so 045. Drift is 4 miles over 4 hours = 1 knot. B is the reciprocal and C forgot the hours.

=== lesson F1 ===

  F1  What a sight actually measures
  ------------------------------------

  A sextant measures one thing: the angle between a body in the sky and
  your horizon. Nothing else. It does not know where you are, and it does
  not care.

    1. You bring the body down to the horizon in the mirrors and read the
       angle off the arc. That reading is Hs, the sextant altitude.
    2. Straight overhead is 90 degrees. The horizon is 0. So an altitude of
       40 degrees means the body stands 40 degrees up from the sea.
    3. The useful quantity is the other half of that: 90 minus the altitude,
       called the zenith distance. It is how far the body is from being
       directly overhead.

  That last number is the whole trick, and the next two lessons are about
  why. Hold on to it: zenith distance = 90 - altitude.

       zenith (straight up)
           |
           |      90 - Hs = zenith distance
           |   .-'
           | .'        * the body
           |'      .-'
           |   .-'   Hs = the angle you measure
        you o------------------------------  your horizon


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. You measure an altitude of 62 degrees. What is the zenith distance?
       a) 62 degrees      b) 28 degrees      c) 152 degrees

=== answer F1 (given b) ===

  Correct.  90 - 62 = 28 degrees, which is 1680 miles from the GP.

=== lesson F2 ===

  F2  The geographical position
  -------------------------------

  At any instant, every body in the sky is directly overhead somewhere on
  the earth. That spot is its geographical position, or GP.

    1. The GP has a latitude and a longitude, like any other place.
    2. Its latitude is the body's declination - how far north or south of
       the celestial equator the body lies.
    3. Its longitude comes from the body's Greenwich Hour Angle, GHA -
       how far west of Greenwich it has swung.
    4. The GP moves, and quickly: the earth turns 15 degrees of longitude
       an hour, so a body's GP travels about 900 nautical miles westward
       every hour at the equator.

  The almanac exists to answer one question: at this instant of Universal
  Time, where is this body's GP? Declination gives the latitude, GHA gives
  the longitude. That is all an almanac is for.

  Nothing here depends on where you are. The GP is the same for every
  observer on earth at that instant, which is exactly what makes it useful.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. What does a body's declination tell you?
       a) the latitude of its GP   b) the longitude of its GP   c) its altitude

=== answer F2 (given b) ===

  Not quite - the answer is A.  Declination is the latitude of the GP; GHA gives the longitude.

=== lesson F3 ===

  F3  Your altitude puts you on a circle
  ----------------------------------------

  Now put the two ideas together.

    1. If a body were exactly overhead, its altitude would be 90 degrees
       and you would be standing on its GP.
    2. Measure an altitude of 50 degrees instead, and your zenith distance
       is 40 degrees. You are 40 degrees away from the GP.
    3. One degree on the earth is 60 nautical miles, so you are 2400 miles
       from the GP - but the sextant says nothing about which direction.
    4. Every point 2400 miles from the GP is a candidate. Those points form
       a circle drawn on the earth, centred on the GP.

  That circle is your circle of position. One sight, one circle. You are
  somewhere on it.

  Q. A sight gives a zenith distance of 30 degrees. How far are you from the GP?
       a) 30 miles        b) 300 miles       c) 1800 miles

=== lesson F4 ===

  F4  Two circles give a fix
  ----------------------------

  One circle is not a position. Two are.

    1. Take a second sight of a different body. It gives a second circle,
       centred on that body's GP.
    2. Two circles on a sphere cross in two places, and you are at one of
       them.
    3. The two crossings are usually hundreds or thousands of miles apart,
       so your dead reckoning tells you at a glance which one is yours.
    4. A third sight is the check. If all three circles pass through the
       same small area, the round was a good one. If they enclose a large
       triangle, one of the sights is wrong.

  That is celestial navigation entire. Everything after this lesson is
  about doing it accurately and quickly, on a small chart table, without
  drawing circles thousands of miles across.

=== lesson F5 ===

  F5  Why we never draw the circles
  -----------------------------------

  A circle of position is typically two or three thousand miles across.
  You cannot draw one on a chart. You do not need to.

    1. You already know roughly where you are - your dead reckoning.
    2. Near your DR, a stretch of that vast circle is almost perfectly
       straight. Over 60 miles it departs from a straight line by a
       fraction of a mile.
    3. So instead of the circle, we draw a short straight line: the line
       of position, or LOP.
    4. To place it we need two things - how far the circle passes from
       the DR, and in what direction. Those are the intercept and the
       azimuth, and computing them is what sight reduction does.

  The picture below is the circle from lesson F3, zoomed in to a 60-mile
  stretch, with the vertical stretched so that you can see the curve at all.

=== lesson T1 ===

  T1  Universal Time, and why the clock rules
  ---------------------------------------------

  Everything in celestial navigation is referred to Universal Time, which
  for our purposes is the same as GMT. Not ship's time, not local time.

    1. The earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours: 15 degrees an hour,
       15 minutes of arc a minute, 15 seconds of arc a second.
    2. At the equator one minute of arc of longitude is one nautical mile.
       So one second of clock error is a quarter of a mile of longitude.
    3. Four seconds is a mile. Forty seconds is ten miles.
    4. A clock error moves your longitude and leaves your latitude alone,
       which is a useful thing to recognise: if a fix is wrong east-west
       but right north-south, suspect the time before anything else.

  The practical rules that follow from this:

    - Set your watch against a known source and write down the error and
      the date you checked it.
    - Note the time of a sight to the second, at the instant the body
      touches the horizon.
    - If you are shooting alone, call the time out loud as you take it, or
      take the sight and then read the watch - never the other way round.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. Your watch is 20 seconds fast and you do not allow for it. How wrong is the fix?
       a) 5 miles of longitude   b) 20 miles of longitude   c) 5 miles of latitude

=== answer T1 (given b) ===

  Not quite - the answer is A.  Four seconds is one mile, so 20 seconds is five - and it is longitude, not latitude.

=== lesson T2 ===

  T2  GHA and declination
  -------------------------

  These two numbers are the GP, and the almanac's whole job is to produce
  them for a given instant.

    1. Declination is the latitude of the GP. North is positive, south
       negative. The sun's declination swings between about 23.4 degrees
       north in June and 23.4 south in December, and it is what gives us
       seasons.
    2. Greenwich Hour Angle is the longitude of the GP, measured westward
       from Greenwich, and always written 0 to 360 degrees rather than
       east and west.
    3. To convert: a GHA of 45 degrees is longitude 45 degrees west. A GHA
       of 300 degrees is longitude 60 degrees east, because 360 - 300 = 60.
    4. GHA increases by about 15 degrees an hour for every body, because it
       is really a measure of the earth's rotation.

  In CELNAV, 'celnav alm' prints GHA and declination for any body at any
  time. You can check it against a printed Nautical Almanac page and the
  figures will agree to a fraction of a minute of arc.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. A body has GHA 300 degrees. What longitude is its GP in?
       a) 300 degrees west   b) 60 degrees east   c) 60 degrees west

=== answer T2 (given b) ===

  Correct.  GHA is measured west, so 300 west is the same place as 60 east.

=== lesson T3 ===

  T3  Aries and SHA: why the stars are different
  ------------------------------------------------

  The sun, moon and planets each get their own GHA in the almanac. The
  stars are handled differently, and for a good reason.

    1. The stars are so far away that they do not move relative to one
       another in any human timescale. The whole star sphere turns as one
       rigid thing.
    2. So the almanac gives the hour angle of one imaginary point, the
       First Point of Aries, and then each star's fixed offset from it.
    3. That offset is the Sidereal Hour Angle, SHA, measured westward from
       Aries. Sirius has an SHA of about 259 degrees and will still have
       it in twenty years.
    4. So: GHA of a star = GHA Aries + SHA, subtracting 360 if needed.

  One consequence worth knowing: the star sphere gains about four minutes
  a day on the sun. A star that crosses your meridian at 2000 tonight will
  do it at 1956 tomorrow, and two hours earlier in a month.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. How do you get the GHA of a star?
       a) it is tabulated for each star   b) GHA Aries plus the star's SHA
       c) GHA of the sun plus the star's SHA

=== answer T3 (given b) ===

  Correct.  One hour angle for the whole star sphere, plus each star's fixed offset.

=== lesson T4 ===

  T4  LHA: the angle at the pole
  --------------------------------

  GHA is measured from Greenwich. What matters to you is the angle measured
  from your own meridian, and that is the Local Hour Angle.

    1. LHA = GHA + your longitude, counting east longitude as positive and
       west as negative, then brought back into the range 0 to 360.
    2. LHA 0 means the body is exactly on your meridian - due north or due
       south of you, and at its highest for the day. That is the noon sight.
    3. LHA 90 means the body is a quarter of the way round the sky to the
       west of you; LHA 270 means a quarter of the way to the east.
    4. LHA is the angle at the pole in the navigational triangle, which is
       the shape the whole reduction is built on. You meet it again in R1.

  Worked example. GHA of the sun is 283 degrees 42 minutes; you are in
  longitude 40 degrees west. LHA = 283 42 - 40 00 = 243 42. The sun is
  243 degrees west of you round the sky - which is another way of saying
  116 degrees to the east of you, and therefore still in the morning sky.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------

       a) 180 degrees     b) 240 degrees     c) 330 degrees

=== answer T4 (given b) ===

  Correct.  LHA = GHA + longitude east = 210 + 30 = 240 degrees.

=== lesson T5 ===

  T5  Using the almanac in CELNAV
  ---------------------------------

  CELNAV computes the almanac rather than tabulating it, so there is no
  year to run out and no page to turn.

    1. 'celnav alm "2026-08-29 07:30:00" sun,moon,venus' prints GHA,
       declination, semi-diameter and horizontal parallax for those bodies.
    2. Add a star by name - 'celnav alm "..." Dubhe' - and it also prints
       the SHA, so you can cross-check against a printed almanac.
    3. 'celnav stars' lists all 57 navigational stars plus Polaris with
       their SHA and declination for the date.
    4. GHA Aries is printed at the top of every almanac page, so you can
       verify the star relation from T3 by hand.

  A good habit while you are learning: work a sight from a paper almanac,
  then run the same time and body through CELNAV. Where the two disagree
  by more than a couple of tenths of a minute, one of you has made a
  mistake - and finding out which is the most useful hour you can spend.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. Why does CELNAV never need an almanac update?

       c) it downloads them when it can

=== answer T5 (given b) ===

  Correct.  Positions come from orbital theory in the script, so nothing expires.

=== lesson S1 ===

  S1  Taking a sight
  --------------------

  The instrument work is most of the accuracy. The arithmetic that follows
  is exact; your sight is not.

    1. Pre-set roughly. For a star, set the sextant to the altitude you
       expect - CELNAV's planning list gives it - and the star will be near
       the horizon in the mirror when you look along the bearing.
    2. Bring the body down to the horizon, then rock the sextant gently
       side to side. The body swings in an arc; the lowest point of that
       arc is true vertical. Touch it to the horizon there.
    3. Sun: bring the lower limb - the bottom edge - to sit on the horizon.
       Use the shades. Never look at the sun without them.
    4. Note the time at the instant of contact, then read the arc.

  Three practical points that matter more than they sound:

    - Take three or four sights of the same body in quick succession and
      keep them all. Their scatter tells you what your sights are worth.
    - Shoot from as high as you can safely stand, and know that height.
    - In a seaway, wait for the top of the roll so that you see a true
      horizon rather than the back of a wave.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. Why do you rock the sextant while taking a sight?
       a) to steady your hand   b) to find true vertical - the lowest point of the swing
       c) to clear the mirrors

=== answer S1 (given b) ===

  Correct.  A sextant not held vertical always reads too high. The bottom of the arc is vertical.

=== lesson S2 ===

  S2  Index error and dip
  -------------------------

  Two corrections come off before anything else. Both are about you and
  your instrument, not about the sky.

    INDEX ERROR is the sextant reading when it should read zero.
    1. Set the sextant to zero and look at the horizon. If the two images
       do not line up, the difference is the index error.
    2. If the reading is on the arc - a positive reading - the error is
       subtracted. Off the arc, it is added. Check it every day; it moves.

    DIP is because you are not at sea level.
    3. From a height, the visible horizon is slightly below true
       horizontal, so every altitude you measure is slightly too big.
    4. Dip in minutes of arc is about 1.76 times the square root of your
       height of eye in metres. From 3 metres that is 3.0 minutes - three
       miles of error if you forget it.

  Take both off Hs and you have Ha, the apparent altitude.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. Your height of eye is 9 metres. Roughly what is the dip?
       a) 1.8 minutes     b) 5.3 minutes     c) 9 minutes

=== answer S2 (given b) ===

  Correct.  1.76 times the square root of 9 = 1.76 x 3 = 5.3 minutes, always subtracted.

=== lesson S3 ===

  S3  Refraction
  ----------------

  The atmosphere bends light downwards as it comes in, so every body
  appears higher than it really is. Refraction is always subtracted.

    1. It depends almost entirely on altitude. At 45 degrees it is one
       minute of arc. At 20 degrees, about 2.6. At 10 degrees, 5.3. At the
       horizon it reaches about 34 minutes - more than the sun's diameter.
    2. That is why the setting sun you can see is already, geometrically,
       below the horizon.
    3. Temperature and pressure change it by a few per cent. CELNAV asks
       for both; they matter for low sights and are almost irrelevant
       above 25 degrees.
    4. Below about 15 degrees, refraction depends on the real temperature
       profile between you and the horizon, which no formula knows. That is
       the reason low sights are less trustworthy, not the sextant.

  Practical rule: prefer bodies between 15 and 70 degrees. Above 70 the
  azimuth changes quickly with position; below 15 the refraction is a guess.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. Which sight is least affected by uncertainty in refraction?
       a) a body at 8 degrees   b) a body at 25 degrees   c) a body at 60 degrees

=== answer S3 (given b) ===

  Not quite - the answer is C.  Refraction shrinks fast with altitude - about 0.6 minutes at 60 degrees.

=== lesson S4 ===

  S4  Semi-diameter and parallax
  --------------------------------

  Two more corrections, needed only for the sun and moon.

    SEMI-DIAMETER, because the sun and moon are discs, not points.
    1. You measured an edge; you want the centre. The sun's semi-diameter
       is about 16 minutes, the moon's about 15 to 16.
    2. Lower limb: add it. Upper limb: subtract it. Stars and planets are
       points, so there is nothing to do.

    PARALLAX, because you are on the surface of the earth, not at its
    centre, and the almanac gives positions as seen from the centre.
    3. For the sun this is at most 0.15 minutes - one sixth of a mile, and
       usually ignorable but free to include.
    4. For the moon it is enormous: up to about 61 minutes, a full degree.
       A moon sight worked without parallax is 60 miles wrong. It is
       largest when the moon is on the horizon and zero when overhead,
       which is why the correction carries a cosine of the altitude.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. You take an upper-limb sight of the sun. What do you do with semi-diameter?
       a) add about 16 minutes   b) subtract about 16 minutes   c) ignore it

=== answer S4 (given b) ===

  Correct.  Upper limb subtract, lower limb add - you are correcting to the centre.

=== lesson S5 ===

  S5  The whole chain, and the size of each part
  ------------------------------------------------

  In order, always: Hs, then index error and dip to get Ha, then refraction,
  parallax and semi-diameter to get Ho. Ho is what a perfect observer at the
  centre of the earth would have measured, and it is the number the
  reduction uses.

  Below is a real sun sight with every correction drawn to scale, so you
  can see which ones actually matter.

  A sun sight: Hs 34 12.0', lower limb, index error 1.5' on the arc,
  height of eye 4 m, 15 C, 1013 mb.

    Hs  sextant reading       34 12.0'
    index error                -1.5'  =
    dip (height of eye)        -3.5'  ==
    Ha  apparent altitude     34 07.0'
    refraction                 -1.4'  =
    parallax                   +0.1'  
    semi-diameter             +15.9'  =========
    Ho  observed altitude     34 21.6'

  Semi-diameter and dip dominate; refraction is small at this altitude;
  the sun's parallax is almost nothing. For the moon the picture is
  entirely different - parallax alone can be a whole degree.

  Q. Which correction is by far the largest for a low moon sight?
       a) refraction      b) parallax        c) dip

  Correct.  The moon's parallax reaches about 61 minutes - over a degree, or 60 miles.

=== lesson R1 ===

  R1  The assumed position and the navigational triangle
  --------------------------------------------------------

  You cannot compute your position directly. You compute what a sight
  would have looked like from a position you assume, and then compare.

    1. Take an assumed position - in CELNAV, your DR itself.
    2. Draw three points on the sky as seen from there: your zenith Z
       straight overhead, the elevated pole P, and the body itself.
    3. Those three points make a spherical triangle, and every quantity in
       sight reduction is one of its parts:

         side Z to P    = 90 - your latitude          (co-latitude)
         side P to body = 90 - the declination        (polar distance)
         side Z to body = 90 - the altitude           (zenith distance)
         angle at P     = LHA
         angle at Z     = the azimuth of the body

    4. You know the first two sides and the angle between them, so the
       triangle is fully determined. Solving it gives the third side and
       the angle at Z - which is to say, the altitude and bearing the body
       would have had from your assumed position.

=== lesson R2 ===

  R2  Hc and Zn
  ---------------

  Solving that triangle gives two numbers, and CELNAV prints both.

    1. Hc, the computed altitude:

           sin Hc = sin(lat) sin(dec) + cos(lat) cos(dec) cos(LHA)

    2. Zn, the true bearing of the body from the assumed position, taken
       from the same triangle and given as 000 to 360 degrees true.
    3. Hc is what you would have measured if you really had been at the
       assumed position. Ho is what you did measure. They differ because
       you are not there.
    4. Zn is the direction of the body's GP from you - which is also the
       direction in which the circle of position runs away from you, and
       therefore the direction along which the whole comparison is made.

  Two lines of trigonometry replace a book of tables. The tables were only
  ever a way of doing this arithmetic without a calculator.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. What is Hc?
       a) the altitude you measured   b) the altitude you would have measured
          from the assumed position   c) the corrected sextant reading

=== answer R2 (given b) ===

  Correct.  Hc comes entirely from the assumed position and the almanac - your sextant plays no part in it.

=== lesson R3 ===

  R3  The intercept: toward or away
  -----------------------------------

  Here is where the sight finally tells you something about your position.

    1. The intercept is simply Ho minus Hc, in minutes of arc - which are
       nautical miles.
    2. A bigger altitude means you are closer to the GP. So if Ho is
       greater than Hc, you are nearer the body than the assumed position
       was: the intercept is TOWARD, in the direction Zn.
    3. If Ho is less than Hc, you are further away: AWAY, in the opposite
       direction.
    4. The old mnemonic is 'computed greater, away' - if Hc is the greater
       of the two, plot away from the body.

  Example. Ho is 19 degrees 24.8 minutes, Hc is 19 degrees 23.3 minutes.
  The difference is 1.5 minutes, so 1.5 miles, and Ho is the larger:
  1.5 miles TOWARD the body along its azimuth.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. Ho is 40 12.0' and Hc is 40 19.0'. What do you plot?
       a) 7 miles toward the body   b) 7 miles away from the body
       c) 31 miles toward the body

=== answer R3 (given b) ===

  Correct.  Hc is the greater, so away: computed greater, away. The difference is 7.0 minutes = 7 miles.

=== lesson R4 ===

  R4  Plotting the line of position
  -----------------------------------

  Three steps, and they are the same whether you use paper or the screen.

    1. Mark the assumed position.
    2. Draw the azimuth line from it, in the direction Zn.
    3. Measure the intercept along that line - toward the body or away from
       it - and mark the point. That point is on your circle of position.
    4. Through that point draw a line at right angles to the azimuth. That
       is the line of position. You are somewhere on it.

  Why at right angles: the azimuth points at the GP, and the circle of
  position is centred on the GP, so the circle - and the straight line we
  use in its place - must cross the azimuth square on.

  On CELNAV's plot the assumed position is the + at the centre, the dotted
  line is the azimuth, the letter marks the end of the intercept, and the
  lettered line through it is the LOP.

=== lesson R5 ===

  R5  Crossing, running, and knowing when to doubt it
  -----------------------------------------------------

    CROSSING. Two LOPs from bodies well apart in azimuth cross at your
    position. Three give you a check: a small triangle means good sights,
    a large one means at least one is wrong.

    RUNNING. Sights taken minutes or hours apart were taken from different
    places, because the boat moved. Each earlier LOP is advanced along the
    course made good by the distance run, and the fix is taken from the
    advanced lines. Set course and speed and CELNAV does this for you; the
    classic case is the sun line in the morning advanced to noon.

    DOUBTING. Two things tell you how much to trust a fix:
    1. The residuals - how far each LOP misses the final answer. This is
       your observing error, and under a mile is good work from a small
       boat.
    2. The geometry - how far one minute of sight error moves the fix.
       Bodies bunched in one quarter of the sky give a long thin error
       ellipse and a confident-looking fix that is badly wrong along one
       axis. CELNAV prints this in miles and warns you when the cut is weak.

    Bodies about 120 degrees apart in azimuth give the tightest fix, which
    is why the planning list suggests three with the widest spread.

=== lesson L1 ===

  L1  The noon sight: latitude without a reduction
  --------------------------------------------------

  Everything in modules R and S was about a sight taken at any old
  moment, from a position you only guessed. There is one moment in the
  day when none of that is needed.

  At local apparent noon the sun crosses your meridian. Its LHA is
  zero. Look at what that does to the navigational triangle:

    1. The angle at the pole IS the LHA, and it has gone to zero. A
       triangle with no angle at one corner is not a triangle: the
       three points lie on one great circle, your own meridian.
    2. So the azimuth has no east-west component at all. The sun bears
       due south of you, or due north, and nothing in between.
    3. A line of position runs square across the azimuth. Square across
       due south is due east and west - a parallel of latitude.

  That is why this sight needs no assumed position and no intercept.
  You are not comparing what you measured with what you would have
  measured from somewhere else. You are measuring your latitude.

  The whole method:

       zenith distance  =  90 - Ho
       latitude         =  declination  +/-  zenith distance

  and the sign is only which side of you the sun stood: PLUS the
  zenith distance if it bore south of you, MINUS if it bore north.

  Three things to know about the moment itself.

    4. It is not noon by your watch, and it is not noon by local mean
       time either. Two corrections stand between: your longitude
       (four minutes of time per degree) and the equation of time,
       which runs from about 14 minutes behind the clock in February
       to 16 minutes ahead of it in November.
    5. You do not need to know it in advance. The altitude is at a
       maximum, so it is momentarily standing still - it changes as
       the SQUARE of the time either side. Follow the sun up, catch
       the highest reading it gives you, and watch it start to fall.
       The reading is what you want; the clock is not the point.
    6. That is the opposite of every other sight you have taken. Four
       seconds of clock error moves a star sight a mile. Here it moves
       almost nothing, which is why this sight was the backbone of
       navigation for two centuries.

  And the honest limit. One sight, one line, and that line is a
  parallel of latitude: it is true on its own and needs no crossing,
  but it tells you NOTHING about your longitude. For that you need a
  second body, or a second sight after a run, or the old trick of
  equal altitudes either side of noon.

    celnav noon                      the moment, and what to expect
    celnav noon "2026-10-21" <Hs>    the latitude from your reading

            Z    your zenith - and its height above the equator
            |    IS your latitude, which is what you want
            |
            |    90 - Ho, the zenith distance: how far the sun
            |    stands from straight overhead
            |
            *    the sun, on your meridian, bearing due south
            |
            |    its declination, straight out of the almanac
     - - - -+- - - -   the celestial equator

  Read it off the diagram: from the equator up to your zenith is your
  latitude, and that same span is the declination plus the zenith
  distance. There is nothing else in it.

  A worked one. Falmouth, 50 09'N 005 04'W, on 21 October 2026:

    the sun crosses your meridian at   12:04:54 UT
    its declination then is            10 48.1' S
    so expect a meridian altitude of   29.0 degrees, bearing south
    measure that, and 90 - Ho = 60 57.1' of zenith distance,
    which minus 10 48.1' of southerly declination puts you
    back on 50 09.0'.

  Q. At local apparent noon your zenith distance is 60 57.0', the sun's

=== lesson L2 ===

  L2  Polaris: latitude from one star, all night long
  -----------------------------------------------------

  The noon sight gives you latitude at one moment of the day. There is
  another that gives it to you any time the horizon is visible, and it
  needs even less arithmetic.

  The pole of the sky has no star on it. But one lies close: Polaris,
  a little over half a degree off. Here is why that matters.

    1. The altitude of the celestial pole above your horizon IS your
       latitude. Exactly, always, by the geometry of the thing - stand
       on the equator and the pole is on your horizon; stand at the
       pole and it is overhead.
    2. So if Polaris sat ON the pole, you would measure your latitude
       directly off the sextant, with nothing to correct and no
       almanac to open.
    3. It does not. It circles the pole once a day at that half a
       degree and a bit, so it stands sometimes above the pole and
       sometimes below it and mostly to one side.

  The whole correction is therefore one question: where on that little
  circle is Polaris right now? And its LHA answers it.

       at upper transit, above the pole:   latitude = Ho - p
       at lower transit, below the pole:   latitude = Ho + p
       anywhere between:                   latitude = Ho - p cos LHA

  where p is the polar distance, 90 minus the declination.

    4. The almanac prints this as Ho - 1 degree + a0 + a1 + a2. Do not
       let the three columns suggest three ideas: they are one
       correction, written as a series. a0 carries almost all of it
       and depends on the LHA of Aries; a1 is a small term in your
       latitude and a2 a smaller one in the month. The constant degree
       is subtracted only so that all three columns can be printed as
       positive numbers.
    5. CELNAV does not need the series. It has Polaris's own GHA and
       declination, so it solves the triangle the star is standing in
       and gets the answer in closed form. Ask it for a sight and it
       prints both: the first-order correction above, and the exact
       one, so you can see the size of what a1 and a2 are for.

  Two honest limits, and the first is not small.

    6. There is no Polaris in the southern hemisphere. Nothing bright
       sits near the south pole - Sigma Octantis is a degree off and
       barely visible - so this method simply does not exist below the
       equator, and a noon sight does the work instead.
    7. It is one line, a parallel of latitude, and tells you nothing
       about your longitude. What it does give you is time: Polaris
       is up all night, so you are not waiting for a particular
       moment the way you are at noon.

  And a long slow caveat worth knowing: Polaris has not always been
  the pole star and will not stay one. The earth's axis precesses, so
  the pole is drifting through the sky - Polaris is closing on it now,
  will be at its nearest around 2100, and in two thousand years will
  be nowhere near it. That is why the star catalogue in this program
  carries proper motion and precession rather than fixed positions.

    celnav polaris                   the correction right now
    celnav polaris "UT" <Hs>         the latitude from your reading

              Polaris goes round the pole once a day:

                        upper transit
                             *   latitude = Ho - p
                          .     .
                        .         .
                *       .    + P   .       *
            latitude    .         .     latitude
            = Ho        .         .     = Ho
                          .     .
                             *   latitude = Ho + p
                        lower transit

    P = the celestial pole, whose altitude is your latitude.
    p = Polaris's distance from it, about 37' at present.

  Out at the sides, Polaris is level with the pole and its altitude IS
  your latitude. Above and below, it is wrong by the whole of p. In
  between, by the part of p that lies along your meridian, which is
  p cos LHA - and that is the correction, all of it.

  Tonight, for instance, from 005 04'W at 20:00 UT:

    Polaris GHA 284 03.8', so its LHA is 278 59.8'
    its polar distance is 37.5'
    so the correction to Ho is -5.9'

  Q. You take Polaris at its lower transit - directly below the pole -

=== answer L2 (given b) ===

=== lesson B1 ===

  B1  The moon: where the corrections stop being bookkeeping
  ------------------------------------------------------------

  Module S walked the correction chain on a sun sight, and every term
  in it was small: a couple of minutes of dip, two of refraction,
  sixteen of semi-diameter, and a parallax of a sixth of a minute you
  could have ignored and never noticed.

  The moon is the body that makes the chain matter.

    1. PARALLAX. The corrections you apply are all about turning what
       YOU saw into what an observer at the centre of the earth would
       have seen, because that is where the almanac stands. For the
       sun, 93 million miles away, the earth's radius is nothing. The
       moon is a quarter of a million miles away, so the earth's
       radius is a real fraction of that, and the correction reaches
       about 61 minutes - a whole degree, sixty miles of position.
       It is not a refinement. It is the biggest number in the sum.
    2. SEMI-DIAMETER, and it GROWS as the moon rises. You measure to
       a limb, so you add or subtract the moon's half-width - but when
       the moon is overhead you are one earth radius nearer to it than
       when it is on your horizon, so it looks very slightly bigger.
       That is augmentation, and it is about 0.3' at the zenith.
       CELNAV applies it; most tables fold it into the main
       correction.
    3. WHICH LIMB. Half the month the lower limb is the lit one and
       half the month it is not, so a moon sight is the one sight
       where you routinely shoot the upper limb - and the
       semi-diameter changes sign when you do. Get that wrong and you
       are out by twice the semi-diameter, about half a degree, thirty
       miles.

  Then the thing that catches people out, which is not a correction at
  all:

    4. The moon moves about its own diameter in an hour. Half a degree
       an hour is half a minute of arc a minute of time, so four
       seconds of clock error is a tenth of a mile and a minute of it
       is half a mile. Every sight wants the time to the second; this
       one punishes you for less.

  And the reason to bother with all of it:

    5. The moon is the only body besides the sun that is regularly up
       in DAYLIGHT. A sun line crossed with a moon line is the one
       two-body fix available at midday, when there is no twilight to
       wait for and a running fix would mean two hours of hoping your
       course and speed were honest. 'celnav plan' lists the moon
       with its phase, and will say so when it is up with the sun.

  A sun sight first - the same one module S5 works through:

    Hs  sextant reading       34 12.0'
    index error                -1.5'  =
    dip (height of eye)        -3.5'  ==
    Ha  apparent altitude     34 07.0'
    refraction                 -1.4'  =
    parallax                   +0.1'  
    semi-diameter             +15.9'  =========
    Ho  observed altitude     34 21.6'

  And a moon sight at the same height of eye, with the moon's own
  figures for tonight out of the almanac:

    (semi-diameter 15.5', horizontal parallax 57.0')

    Hs  sextant reading       34 12.0'
    index error                -1.5'  =
    dip (height of eye)        -3.5'  ==
    Ha  apparent altitude     34 07.0'
    refraction                 -1.4'  =
    parallax                  +47.2'  ==========================
    semi-diameter             +15.7'  =========
    Ho  observed altitude     35 08.4'

  Same sextant reading, same horizon, same air. The sun's corrections
  very nearly cancel; the moon's do not come close. Read the bars
  rather than the numbers: that one long row is why a moon sight
  reduced with the sun's habits is wrong by tens of miles.

  Q. You shoot the moon's UPPER limb but reduce it as a lower-limb sight.

=== lesson B2 ===

  B2  The planets: a point of light that moves
  ----------------------------------------------

  After the moon, the planets are a relief. There are four a navigator
  uses - Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn - and in the sextant they behave
  almost exactly like stars.

    1. NO SEMI-DIAMETER. A planet shows a disc only in a telescope; in
       a sextant it is a point, so you bring the point itself to the
       horizon and there is no limb to choose and no half-width to add.
       That alone removes the two corrections that cause most mistakes
       on the sun and the moon.
    2. ALMOST NO PARALLAX - but not quite none, and this program used
       to call it none. Venus comes within 0.27 of the earth's own
       distance from the sun, and at that range its parallax reaches
       0.5 minutes of arc near the horizon: half a mile. Mars reaches
       0.2'. Jupiter and Saturn, four and eight times further out,
       give three hundredths of a minute and can be forgotten. The
       almanac prints an extra correction for Venus and Mars for
       exactly this reason; CELNAV computes it for all four.

  What a planet does have, and a star does not, is motion of its own.
  That is where the almanac's two odd little columns come from:

    3. The almanac tabulates GHA and declination for each whole hour,
       and you interpolate for the minutes and seconds. For a star
       that works perfectly, because a star's GHA advances at the same
       rate every hour for ever - the rate the earth turns.
    4. A planet is also moving around the sun while you watch, so its
       GHA does NOT advance at quite that rate, and the rate itself
       changes through the year. The 'v' correction is the difference,
       and 'd' is the same idea for the declination.
    5. So v and d are not physics. They are the repair work that
       tabulating by the hour makes necessary. CELNAV has no need of
       either: it computes the position for the second you ask about,
       from the orbit, so there is nothing to interpolate and nothing
       to correct for having interpolated.

  And the practical reason to care about any of this:

    6. The planets are BRIGHT. Venus at magnitude -4 and Jupiter at
       -2 are far brighter than any star, so they show while the
       horizon is still crisp - before the stars come out in the
       evening and after they have faded in the morning. That widens
       the twilight window at both ends, which is the scarcest thing
       in celestial navigation.
    7. Venus can be found in broad daylight if you know where to
       look, which is the one thing 'celnav plan' is for: it lists
       every planet that is up with its altitude, azimuth and
       magnitude, so you can point the sextant at empty-looking sky
       and find it there.

  Vega, at a sextant reading of 20 degrees:

    Hs  sextant reading       20 00.0'
    index error                -1.5'  =
    dip (height of eye)        -3.5'  ==
    Ha  apparent altitude     19 55.0'
    refraction                 -2.7'  =
    Ho  observed altitude     19 52.3'

  And Venus, same reading, same horizon:

    (a point source, so no semi-diameter; horizontal parallax 0.54')

    Hs  sextant reading       20 00.0'
    index error                -1.5'  =
    dip (height of eye)        -3.5'  ==
    Ha  apparent altitude     19 55.0'
    refraction                 -2.7'  =
    parallax                   +0.5'  
    Ho  observed altitude     19 52.8'

  One row of difference, and it is a small one - which is the point.
  A planet is a star you can shoot earlier in the evening. The work
  that makes planets awkward is all in the almanac, and none of it is
  in the sextant.


  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. The almanac prints a “v” correction for the planets and none for the
     stars. What is it for?
       a) the planet's semi-diameter   b) the planet's own motion, which
          makes its GHA advance at a slightly different rate each hour
       c) its parallax

=== answer B2 (given b) ===

  Correct.  A star's GHA advances at the rate the earth turns, every hour, for ever. A planet is moving in its own orbit as well, so the hourly rate differs and changes through the year. v is the repair that tabulating by the hour makes necessary - CELNAV computes the position for the second and needs neither v nor d.

  C1  Choosing your bodies: the cut matters more than the sights
  ----------------------------------------------------------------

  A fix is only as good as the angles between its lines. Three careful
  sights of three bodies in the same quarter of the sky make a worse
  fix than three rough sights spread right round you.

    1. A line of position runs square across the azimuth of its body.
       Two lines crossing at a right angle pin a position in both
       directions at once.
    2. Two lines crossing at fifteen degrees pin it in one direction
       and leave it almost free in the other. One minute of sextant
       error slides the crossing four miles along that weak axis.
    3. So the number to weigh before you shoot is the azimuth spread,
       not the magnitude. Want bodies 90 to 120 degrees apart, and
       three of them rather than two.

  Then the altitude. A body wants to stand between about 15 and 65
  degrees up.

    4. Below 15 degrees the light has come a long way through the
       lowest air, and refraction there depends on the real air and
       sea temperatures, which you do not know. The correction stops
       being arithmetic and becomes a guess.
    5. Above about 65 the azimuth swings quickly - the body is on its
       way to your zenith, where it has no azimuth at all - so a few
       seconds of timing error moves the line. The circle of position
       is tightly curved up there as well, and we draw it straight.

  And the window. A star needs a horizon to measure against and a sky
  dark enough to show it. You get both only between civil and nautical
  twilight, which is around twenty minutes, twice a day.

  CELNAV does this arithmetic for you before you pick up the sextant.

    celnav plan        the twilight times, every body that is up with
                       its altitude, azimuth and magnitude, the ones
                       too low or too high marked as such, the best
                       three by azimuth spread, and a chart of the sky
                       with that set numbered on it

  And afterwards it tells you what the cut was worth. 'celnav fix'
  reports the geometry in miles: what one minute of sight error does
  to the answer, and along which bearing. When it says WEAK CUT, that
  is not a style note - it is the error ellipse, computed from your
  own azimuths. Believe it.

  The order of work is plan, then shoot. Not shoot, then hope.

=== lesson C2 ===

  C2  Checking the compass against the sky
  ------------------------------------------

  Every other compass reference can be wrong. A charted bearing
  assumes you already know where you are. A transit needs two marks in
  sight. Another vessel's heading is her business. The azimuth of a
  star is none of those things: it comes from the almanac and the
  clock, and it is right.

  Three quantities, and it matters which is which:

    1. Variation is the earth's. It is printed on the chart for your
       area and year, and it changes slowly with place and time. It is
       the same for every compass aboard.
    2. Deviation is the ship's. It is the iron in her, and it changes
       with her heading, because the iron turns when she turns.
    3. Compass error is the two together: the whole difference between
       what the card reads and true. A sky check measures the error.
       Take the charted variation off it and what remains is the
       deviation of that compass on that heading.

  The method:

    4. Note the time to the second and read the bearing of the body
       over the compass.
    5. Give CELNAV the instant, the body, the bearing you read and the
       chart variation, west negative:

         celnav compass "2026-10-21 19:00:00" Saturn 88 -14

    6. It computes the true azimuth for that instant, subtracts to get
       the error, and names the deviation that is left.

  Choose a low body, under about 20 degrees. High up the azimuth
  changes fast, so a few seconds of clock error moves it; and a
  bearing taken steeply over a compass card is hard to take honestly.
  A body sitting on the horizon at rising or setting is the classical
  choice, and its azimuth measured off true east or west has its own
  name: the amplitude.

  One honest limit. A check on one heading gives you the deviation on
  that heading and no other. Deviation is a curve around the card, and
  to know the whole of it you swing the ship and check on several
  headings in turn.

  That is what devcard does. Turn the ship onto each heading, take a
  bearing of the body, and note the TIME of each one separately:

    celnav devcard "2026-10-22" sun -2 \
       "000,10:00:00,147.0; 045,10:05:00,146.9; 090,10:10:00,148.4"

  Each bearing keeps its own time because a swing takes twenty minutes
  and the sun moves about five degrees of azimuth in that time. Share
  one time across the whole swing and that nine degrees of the sun's
  own motion lands in the card, looking exactly like deviation.

  It prints the card above, computed from your own swing, and says
  which parts of the compass you did not cover.

  A compass swung on eight headings - the same compass, the same
  ship, the same iron, eight different answers:

       ship's head    deviation      west  <  |  >  east
       ------------   ---------
      000  T         2.2 E               |####
      045  T         4.3 E               |#########
      090  T         3.9 E               |########
      135  T         1.2 E               |##
      180  T         2.3 W          #####|
      225  T         4.3 W      #########|
      270  T         3.9 W       ########|
      315  T         1.2 W             ##|

  One sky check gives you one row of that table. It is the row you
  are steering on, which is the row that matters now, but it is not
  the whole character of the compass. Swing her to learn that.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. A star azimuth check gives a compass error of 6 degrees east, and the
     chart shows variation 10 degrees west. What is the deviation?
       a) 4 degrees east   b) 16 degrees east   c) 16 degrees west

=== answer C2 (given b) ===

  Correct.  Error is variation and deviation together. 6 east with 10 west of variation leaves 16 east of deviation - the ship is pulling the card hard the other way.

  H1  What a sight reduction table actually is
  ----------------------------------------------

  Pub. 249 is four volumes of numbers and no explanation, which makes
  it look like special knowledge. It is not. Every entry in it is the
  same spherical triangle this program solves, worked out in advance
  for whole degrees and printed.

    sin Hc = sin L sin d + cos L cos d cos LHA

  That is the whole of it. Give that formula a latitude, a
  declination and an LHA and it returns an altitude. Pub. 249 is that
  formula evaluated for every whole-degree combination a navigator
  might need, by someone else, before you sailed.

  WHICH IS WHY THIS PROGRAM DOES NOT SHIP THE TABLES. It generates
  them. Restrict the arguments to whole degrees and the engine IS the
  table, so the tutor can print the exact page your sight needs
  instead of the handful of pages an extract would carry.

  THE PRICE OF PRECOMPUTING, which is the thing to understand:

    1. The table only holds WHOLE degrees. So the assumed position
       is not your DR -- it is the whole degree of latitude nearest
       it, and whatever longitude makes the LHA come out whole.
    2. Declination will not be a whole degree. That is what the d
       column is for: how much Hc changes per degree of declination,
       so you can interpolate the minutes yourself.
    3. The azimuth is printed as an ANGLE from the elevated pole, 0
       to 180, not as a true bearing. A rule at the foot of the page
       converts it, and which rule depends on your hemisphere and
       whether the LHA is more or less than 180.

  None of those three is difficulty. They are the bookkeeping, which
  is the whole reason a fix can come out of a book and a pencil with
  no battery anywhere in it.
  A page, generated by this lesson as you read it:

      LAT 50N   DEC 11S   CONTRARY NAME

          LHA          Hc        d      Z
          327    22 31.2'    -55.6    145
          328    22 53.3'    -55.8    146
          329    23 14.8'    -56.0    147
          330    23 35.7'    -56.2    148
          331    23 56.1'    -56.4    149

  Nothing was looked up. Those twenty numbers came out of the same
  line of trigonometry at the top of this lesson, which is all the
  book ever contained.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. Why does a sight reduction table use an assumed position rather than
     your DR?
       a) because the DR is not accurate enough to reduce from
       b) because the table only holds whole degrees, and the AP is
          where the whole degrees are
       c) because the intercept has to be short

=== answer H1 (given b) ===

  Correct.  The AP is a bookkeeping choice, not a judgement about your DR: whole-degree latitude and a longitude that makes the LHA whole, because those are the only rows the book has.

  H2  Volume 1: the seven selected stars
  ----------------------------------------

  Volume 1 is the odd one out and the one worth knowing about.

  It is entered with two arguments only - LHA Aries and your latitude
  - and it gives you Hc and Zn DIRECTLY, for seven stars chosen for
  that moment. No declination argument, no interpolation, no azimuth
  rule. Read seven lines, shoot them, plot them.

  That is possible because the stars do not move relative to Aries
  within the life of an edition, so their declinations can be built
  into the table instead of being an argument to it.

  THE SELECTION IS ITSELF AN ALGORITHM. The seven are chosen bright,
  spread in azimuth, and in the honest altitude band - which is
  exactly what C1 said makes a good set, and exactly what celnav plan
  does when it picks the best three by azimuth spread.

  So the app and the book are solving the same problem. They will
  sometimes disagree, and the disagreement is informative rather than
  a fault: Volume 1 was computed for a whole degree of latitude and a
  whole degree of LHA Aries, for an edition that has an epoch, and it
  does not know what the weather is doing to your horizon tonight.

  Volume 1 also carries the one thing this program cannot: it works
  with no power at all, in a wet cockpit, by torchlight.

=== lesson H3 ===

  H3  Volumes 2 and 3, and the interpolation
  --------------------------------------------

  Volumes 2 and 3 are the general case: any body, any declination
  between 0 and 29 degrees, which covers the sun, the moon, the four
  planets and the stars near the equator.

  Three columns per entry, and each one has a job:

    Hc   the computed altitude at the WHOLE degree of declination
    d    how much Hc changes per whole degree of declination, signed
    Z    the azimuth angle from the elevated pole, 0 to 180

  THE INTERPOLATION IS ONE MULTIPLICATION:

         Hc = Hc(table) + d x (minutes of declination / 60)

  and the sign of d does the thinking for you, as long as you let it.
  A negative d means the altitude FALLS as the declination increases,
  so the correction is subtracted. Applying it the wrong way is the
  commonest error in the method and it is worth twice the correction,
  because you move the wrong way by the amount you should have moved
  the right way.

  THE OTHER TWO MISTAKES, both of which the tutor will name for you:

    * an LHA one degree out. Silent, plausible, and worth about the
      difference between two rows - twenty minutes of arc at a
      workaday altitude, so twenty miles.
    * reading Zn where the page prints Z. The two agree in one
      quadrant and differ by a reflection everywhere else, which is
      the worst kind of error: right often enough to feel safe.

  Work a sight both ways - tables mode in this app does it with you,
  and shows you the exact answer alongside - until the three are
  habits rather than things to remember.
  The interpolation, worked as you read it:

      from the page, LHA 329      Hc 23 14.8'   d -56.0
      declination                 11 07.5'S
      correction  -56.0 x 7.5/60    -7.0'
      interpolated Hc             23 07.8'

      and solved exactly          23 07.8'
      the table costs you         0.0'

  Which is to say: the table has cost you nothing you could plot,
  from arguments of whole degrees. That is what the d column buys,
  and it is why the method survived two hundred years of use at sea.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. The page gives Hc 23 14.8' and d -56.0. Your declination is 11 07.5'S.
     What is the interpolated Hc?
       a) 23 07.8'      b) 23 21.8'      c) 22 18.8'

=== answer H3 (given b) ===

  Not quite - the answer is A.  -56.0 x 7.5/60 = -7.0', so 23 14.8' - 7.0' = 23 07.8'. B added it instead of subtracting, which is worth twice the correction. C used the next whole degree of declination.

  H4  When the tables beat the calculator, and when they do not
  ---------------------------------------------------------------

  An honest comparison, because the answer is not that one is better.

  WHAT THE TABLES HAVE

    They need no power. That is the whole argument and it is enough.
    A book in a dry bag works after the batteries are flat, after the
    lightning strike, and after the water got into the nav station.
    They are also checkable: every number in them is public and the
    same for everyone, so an error in your working is findable by
    someone else looking at the same page.

  WHAT THEY COST

    Whole-degree arguments, so an assumed position that is not where
    you are and an intercept that has to be plotted. Interpolation by
    hand, with three known ways to get it wrong. Volumes that have an
    epoch and go slowly out of date. And time: a tabular reduction is
    minutes of careful arithmetic where this program takes none.

  WHAT THIS PROGRAM HAS

    The exact triangle for the second you asked about, from the DR
    you actually have, with no interpolation and no assumed position.
    And a battery, which is the whole of the case against it.

  THE SENSIBLE POSITION

  Be fluent in both. Work sights by the tables often enough that the
  method is yours, and use the program when it is there - then the
  day the power goes you are not learning the method, you are using
  it. That is the same argument the About page makes about celestial
  navigation against satellites, one level down.

  And one practical note: check the program against the tables, not
  the other way round. If they disagree by more than a minute or two,
  the arguments are wrong somewhere, and the tutor in tables mode
  exists to tell you which one.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Q. What is the one argument for the tables that the calculator cannot
     answer?
       a) they are more accurate
       b) they need no power
       c) they are faster

=== answer H4 (given b) ===

  Correct.  They are less accurate and much slower. They work when nothing else does, which is the only argument that matters and is sufficient on its own.
